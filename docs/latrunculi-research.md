# Ludus Latrunculorum: research report for a multi-variant browser game

**Not verified:**
- Richmond 1994 (Museum Helveticum 51) is gated, so I know it only by citation.
- Murray's 1952 text was not accessible; I have his view only as quoted in Austin's footnotes.
- Vindolanda's individual grid sizes are not published in anything I could read.
- The Pliny (NH 8.215) and Macrobius references are from secondary citation only.
- Bell's "corner square counts as a captor" rule is in Wikipedia and Ludii but not in the Bell 2nd-edition text I read.

## 1. What the ancient sources actually attest

I checked the Latin for every item below except Pollux and Plautus/Lucilius (reported via Schädler and Tilley).

| Source | What it gives |
|---|---|
| Varro, *De lingua Latina* 10.22 | Board is a grid of crossing lines ("ut in tabula solet in qua latrunculis ludunt"), compared to a declension table. Earliest Roman mention. |
| Ovid, *Ars* 2.207–8 | The piece moves "under the image of brigandage"; your soldier dies to a glass enemy (*vitreo … hoste*). |
| Ovid, *Ars* 3.357–60 | One piece perishes to a twin foe (*gemino … hoste*); a warrior caught without his partner fights on; a piece often retraces its path, so backward moves exist. |
| Ovid, *Tristia* 2.477–80 | Differently coloured soldier advances in a straight line (*recto limite*); a piece dies in the middle between two enemies; a following piece can attack and recall the one ahead; a retreating piece is unsafe unaccompanied. |
| Martial 14.17 | Reversible board: dice game one side, the other where a piece "perishes by a twin enemy of another colour". |
| Martial 14.18 | Gem/glass pieces are your "soldier and enemy" in the "wars of ambushing *latrones*". |
| Martial 7.72.7–8 | Opponents "shut in by *mandrae* and a glass *latro*". Meaning of *mandra* (pen, drove) is disputed. |
| *Laus Pisonis* 190–208 | Fullest description, summarised below. Author is anonymous (Bell and Kowalski wrongly say Saleius Bassus). |
| Seneca, *Ep.* 117.30 | Nobody rushing to a house fire studies the board to see how the *alligatus calculus* (bound piece) may get out. So a trapped piece could still escape. |
| Seneca, *De tranq.* 14.7 | Julius Canus, summoned to execution mid-game, counts the pieces and has the centurion witness he is one ahead. So the score was a piece count. |
| Seneca, *Ep.* 106.11 | "latrunculis ludimus" as a byword for over-subtle cleverness. |
| Historia Augusta, *Firmus etc.* 13.2 | Proculus "came out *imperator* ten times" at latrunculi; winner's title. Late and unreliable source. |
| Isidore, *Orig.* 18.67 | Pieces move partly *ordine* (ordinarii), partly *vage* (vagi); those that cannot move are *inciti*. Austin (1934) argued this is about a dice/tabula game, not latrunculi; Schädler uses it. |
| Plautus, Lucilius | Idiom *ad incitas redigere*, reduce to immobility. |
| Pollux 9.98 (Greek *polis*) | Board with cells called "city", pieces "dogs", two colours, capture by enclosing one between two of the other colour. |

*Laus Pisonis* in paraphrase:
- The piece is "varied on the open board in a cleverer way" (*callidiore modo tabula variatur aperta calculus*).
- Wars are fought with glass soldiers; white binds black and black binds white (*alliget*).
- A fleeing piece takes its pursuer; one comes "from a long retreat" from a lookout post.
- One piece accepts a two-sided delay and, seeming bound, binds two (*similisque ligato obligat ipse duos*).
- One bursts through a broken *mandra* to ravage the walls.
- The winner keeps a nearly full phalanx and both hands rattle with captives.

**Securely attested:** gridded board, play on the squares, two colours, glass pieces, custodian capture of a single piece between two enemies on a line, backward movement, a "bound" piece state that can be escaped, captured pieces taken off, win reckoned by piece count, no dice.

**Not attested:** board size, piece count, move distance, starting array, any special piece, corner or edge rules, what happens when moving between two enemies. Schädler says outright that movement and the suicide rule are unknown.

**Names:** *latro*, *latrunculus*, *miles*, *bellator*, *calculus* are poetic synonyms, not piece classes (Schädler; Tilley 1892 already said the officers theory lacked evidence).

## 2. Archaeology

**Board sizes** (Schädler 2001 and 2007; Bell; Ludii):
- Chesters: 7×7, 8×8, 9×10.
- Corbridge: 7×8.
- Richborough: fragments, plus an 8×8 found with 18 discs (11 with concentric circles, 7 plain).
- Dover: 9×10. Chedworth: 8×8. Exeter roof tile: 8×8. Mainz roof tile: 9×9.
- Parthenon steps and Basilica Julia, Rome: 8×8. Smyrna agora: 8×8.
- Samos Heraion: 11×12.
- Athens terracotta group and a Fayum model: 6×7, the latter with 17 pieces in two colours.
- Ludii's evidence database gives the overall range as 6×7 to 14×14.
- Schädler: no standard size, 8×8 commonest; latticed boards are square or n×(n+1).

**Vindolanda:** 16 boards, the largest collection in Roman Britain, of varying size and quality, found near the commander's house, barracks and baths. Housesteads I could not confirm at all.

**Pieces:**
- Glass (hemispherical "plano-convex"), bone, pottery, stone, tile discs.
- Perugia tomb: 816 glass pieces in blue, yellow and white.
- Chester barracks 1924: 21 white, 5 black, 2 blue. Bell guessed the blue were leaders.
- Pompeii painting: yellow, black and white pieces.
- Schädler: thousands of counters show no differentiated piece.

**Stanway "Doctor's grave" (c. AD 40–60):**
- Hinged wooden board about 385×565 mm; grid not preserved.
- 13 blue and 13 white glass counters along the long sides, one white counter smaller, one blue found inverted; no dice.
- Kowalski read it as a 12×8 latrunculi opening with a dux each.
- Schädler (the excavation report) rejects that: the grid could be 8×12 or 9×13, the layout may not be a game position, no Greek or Roman game has a special piece, and if one piece is special the game is probably native British (fidchell/gwyddbwyll type), not latrunculi.

**Dux and dice:** no ancient text mentions a dux, and no dice are associated with the game. The dux is entirely modern (Becq de Fouquières, Bell, Kowalski).

**Relatives:**
- Greek *polis/petteia* has the same capture and is probably the same game family.
- Seega and Kharebga (placement, then custodian capture) were Bell's model.
- Tafl keeps the capture but became asymmetric with a king (Murray, quoted by Austin); derivation is speculative.
- Hasami shogi (Japan) and Mak-yek/Apit-sodok (Siam/Malaya; the latter adds "intervention" capture) are the living analogues; Ming Mang (Tibet) and the Chinese Fang Qi group are further ones.

### Details needed to implement

**Schädler 2001** (Abstract Games 7)
- A trapped piece is called *alligatus* or *incitus* and is turned upside down.
- Removal requires both capturing pieces to be still free.
- A trapped piece is freed at once if one of its two captors is itself trapped.
- His reasoning: Ovid's rescue manoeuvre implies short moves plus leaping; Seneca's passage implies delayed removal.
- Ludii's "Schädler 1994" ruleset is the same without the between-two-enemies clause.

**Locus Ludi Seneca and Piso** (Schädler and Dasen's ERC project, playable online)
- An *incitus* cannot move and cannot act as a captor.
- If one move traps several, the player chooses one to remove and must remove the others on following turns, one per turn.
- Piso drops hopping and the trapped state; several trapped pieces come off together.
- Winner in both is whoever captured more.
- https://locusludi.ch/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/LUDUS-LATRUNCULORUM_rules_GB.pdf
- https://locusludi.ch/play-ancient-online-games/

**Bell** (Board and Table Games from Many Civilizations, 2nd ed., pp. 84–87)
- Explicitly modelled on Seega.
- The dux hop lands on the empty square beyond; it captures only if the landing brackets an enemy against a friendly piece.
- The extra move after a capture can chain.
- Bell admits nothing in the literature supports the dux.
- Ludii ends the game at the 30-move mark with the larger army winning.
- https://archive.org/details/B-001-002-771

**Kowalski** (archived 2008 page)
- 12×8 array: White men a1–l1, dux g2; Black men a8–l8, dux f7 (each dux just right of centre from its owner's side). Black moves first.
- 10×11 alternative: dux in the centre of the back row, five men each side.
- Dux is immobilised when all four sides are blocked; edges and its own men count, provided an enemy completes the block. So one enemy stone can win.
- If the game stalemates, most captures wins.
- He rejects a placement phase, jumping and dice, arguing from Stanway and tafl; Schädler disputes the Stanway reading.
- Kelsey Museum's handout and the reenactor ruleset on RomanArmyTalk are Kowalski's rules.
- https://web.archive.org/web/20081003114509/http://www.aerobiologicalengineering.com/wxk116/Roman/BoardGames/latruncu.html
- https://romanarmytalk.com/rat/printthread.php?tid=23382

**Museum Quintana (Künzing) and Roman Army Museum (Vindolanda Trust)**
- This is the generic "commercial set" ruleset; AncientGameForge uses the same, with the win condition left to agreement.
- https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2b/Museum_Quintana_-_R%C3%A4uberspiel.jpg
- https://romanarmymuseum.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Roman-Games.pdf

**Falkener** (Games Ancient and Oriental, Appendix I)
- Austin called it fanciful; TSR's 1977 "Cohorts" used it.
- https://archive.org/details/GamesAncientAndOriental

**Latrunculi XXI**
- Core rules are Kowalski's plus: illegal to immobilise your own dux; captures optional.
- Extra tactics, added because the author found the classical rook game drawn with good play:
  - pieces adjacent to an enemy are "locked" and may only make capturing or dux-hitting moves;
  - "push and crush" against a wall or a friendly piece;
  - "flank attack" on the rear piece of a line;
  - "phalanx" attack;
  - graded dux mobility by number of sides attacked.
- "Petteia XXI" is the same without duxes.
- https://chessvariants.org/rules/latrunculi-petteia-xxi

**Others**
- Ludii also ships "Seega rules" and "Kharebga rules" rulesets for attested board sizes (6×6 to 11×16): near-full-board placement two per turn, then step (Seega) or slide (Kharebga) moves, capture all to win, pass if blocked.
- navpil's "Poleis": 8×8, 16 each, placement, rook moves; a piece making a non-attacking move flips to *vagus* and may then only make attacking moves. An explicit anti-draw design. https://navpil.github.io/games/petteia_en.html
- Tilley 1892 proposed immobile *inciti* forming a camp; no playable ruleset.
- Austin 1934: partial only. He read *mandra* as any solid phalanx and stressed mutual covering.
- Tsilogiannis et al. 2026 (playtesting study) note that recent work considers the dux unlikely. https://doi.org/10.24072/pcjournal.738

## 4. Strategy and tactics

Sourced points:
- **Cover:** a piece is only capturable on a line where both flanking squares can be reached. Pairs protect one line; 2×2 blocks protect both. Ovid and the *Laus Pisonis* say the same (unaccompanied retreat is unsafe; a piece without its partner must fight alone).
- **Diagonal chains** cannot be captured under rook rules and produce dead draws (Carrillo). A solid defensive row is likewise hard to break (navpil). This is why reconstructions add jumps, a dux, extra moves, move limits or majority scoring.
- **Safe entry as a weapon:** a piece stepping between two enemies threatens both, a fork; the opponent can save one. This is the *Laus Pisonis* "seeming bound, binds two". Carrillo distinguishes direct, passive and combined forks.
- **Corners and edges:** corners are the weakest squares wherever the two-piece corner rule applies. Edges are neutral or safer when walls never capture, since an edge piece can only be taken along the edge.
- **Rook-move play:** pieces control lanes, so maximise coverage before attacking, and get a rover behind the enemy line (Kowalski). In dux games the uncapturable dux is the natural raider and blocker, but must keep an exit; smothering by your own men is the usual loss.
- **Delayed-capture play (Seneca and Schädler):** every capture costs tempo and invites a counter-trap on one of the two captors. Sacrifices to free a bound piece are central.
- **Endgame:** with few pieces the defender can usually avoid capture indefinitely, so material lead plus a count-based end condition decides. Zugzwang and stalemate wins occur where no-move loses.

My own synthesis, for an evaluation function:
- material, heavily weighted;
- pieces capturable in one move, for each side;
- fork count;
- supported-pair and 2×2 counts;
- corner occupancy penalty where the corner rule applies;
- mobility, with a small weight;
- dux free neighbours, as a steep nonlinear term;
- trapped-piece count, in delayed-capture variants.

## 5. Implementations and AI

**Implementations**
- **Ludii** (Digital Ludeme Project): all rulesets above encoded in one `.lud` file, exact and machine-readable, the best implementation reference. https://ludii.games/lud/games/Ludus%20Latrunculorum.lud ; AI code at https://github.com/Ludeme/LudiiAI
- **toddhal/latrunculi:** Python and HTML/JS, minimax, four variants, no licence file. https://github.com/toddhal/latrunculi
- **Mac Latrunculi:** Objective-C, GPLv2, last updated 2013. https://sourceforge.net/projects/mactrunculi/
- A Common Lisp OpenGL version: https://sourceforge.net/projects/latrunculi/
- An itch.io build: https://tailot.itch.io/ludus-latrunculorum-latrunculi

**AI references**
- Closest serious write-up is Schepers' Ming Mang engine (8×8, rook moves, custodian). https://colinschepers.github.io/2019/mingmang
  - Search: negamax alpha-beta with iterative deepening, transposition table, killer and history ordering, null move, late-move reduction, and quiescence on captures.
  - Evaluation: piece count plus a one-move territory measure.
  - Mobility and corner/edge terms made it worse.
  - Speed: depth 6 took about 4 s and depth 8 about 34 s, single core.
- Tafl work reports an effective branching factor around 10–20 with alpha-beta and that simple material-plus-king-safety evaluations play well (Hingston; Tablut student engines, e.g. https://github.com/MatteoFasulo/Tablut).

**My estimates for a browser engine**
- Step-move variants: branching roughly 20–40, so depth 6–8 is feasible in about a second.
- Rook variants: branching roughly 60–100, so depth 4–5 plus capture quiescence.
- Placement phases have 40–64 options per ply; use a pattern heuristic or shallow search there.
- Chained extra moves (Bell) and delayed removal (Seneca) need turn-state in the hash key.
- Always include repetition handling.

## 6. Recommended presets

1. **Legionary** (Quintana / Roman Army Museum): 8×8, 16 each in two rows, rook moves, immediate capture, corner with two, safe entry, capture all or majority after a no-capture limit. **Best for beginners:** no placement, no special piece, instant feedback.
2. **Piso** (Locus Ludi): 8×8, 20 each, placement, one-step moves, immediate capture, most captures wins. The minimal scholarly ruleset.
3. **Seneca** (Locus Ludi / Schädler 2001): Piso plus hops and the bound-then-removed capture with release. **Most historically defensible:** it uses only attested elements (open-board placement, identical pieces, custodian capture, escapable *alligatus*, piece-count result) and comes from the leading specialist. Schädler himself says no ruleset can claim to be the ancient one.
4. **Bell**: 8×7, 16 + jumping capturable dux, placement by twos, extra move on capture, 30-move rule. The classic book version.
5. **Kowalski / Stanway**: 12×8, 12 + uncapturable dux, rook moves, immobilise the dux. The most chess-like and the most widespread online; label the dux and the Stanway link as contested.
6. **Optional — Falkener 1892** (12×12, diagonals) as a curiosity, or **Latrunculi XXI** as an advanced modern variant.

## 7. Rule toggles that genuinely vary between reconstructions

- **Board:** 7×7, 7×8, 8×8, 9×9, 9×10 (all attested); 12×8, 10×11, 12×12 (reconstructors' choices).
- **Pieces per side:** 12–30.
- **Setup:** fixed array (one rank, two ranks, Falkener's alternate squares) or placement (one or two per turn, never with captures).
- **First player:** Black (Kowalski), White (Roman Army Museum), random.
- **Move distance:** one step or rook slide.
- **Directions:** orthogonal only, or eight (Falkener).
- **Jumping:** none; over own; over any; over enemy only; dux only. Single or chained.
- **Capture timing:** immediate, or trap then remove (removal optional and costing the move, or mandatory and free).
- **Trapped pieces as captors:** allowed or not; release when a captor is trapped.
- **Corner capture:** two pieces around the corner. Bell's hostile corner square is unverified (see top).
- **Edges as captors:** off everywhere except Latrunculi XXI's push attack.
- **Multiple captures per move:** all at once, or one per turn.
- **Line capture of several pieces in a row:** Kowalski's early version and Hasami shogi allow it; Schädler says two in a row are safe.
- **Entering between two enemies:** safe; forbidden unless it captures (Schädler 2001); or non-historical Mak-yek intervention capture.
- **Extra move after a capture:** on (Bell, Roman Army Museum) or off.
- **Dux:** none; capturable jumper; uncapturable with immobilisation win. Whether its own men and edges count toward immobilising it; whether a lone dux loses.
- **End conditions:** capture all; reduce to one; opponent has no legal move (loses, or passes as in Kharebga); dux immobilised; no-capture limit (30, larger army wins; or 50, draw); majority of captures.
- **Repetition:** no immediate reversal of the same piece; no repeated position; third repetition illegal, or a loss for the repeater.
